# Literature Review
## Review: Impedance Reduce Control of a Knee Joint Human-Exoskeleton System
*[2019] - Weiguang Huo , Member, IEEE, Samer Mohammed , Member, IEEE, and Yacine Amirat, Member, IEEE*

### **1. Abstract:**  
---

**Obstacles:** 
- exoskeleton mass
- friction
- gravity  
-> all add to human lower limbs 

**Methodology apply:** 
- Nonlinear impedance reduction control (IRC)

**Objective:**  
- Apply to knee joint exoskeleton during flexion/extension movement
- `IRC` approach ensures impedance adaption of the human-exoskeleton system toward a desired level
- `EMG` (electromyography) sensor which is used for estimating human joint torque
> 1. Reduce the required human efforts to achieve a given task while wearing a powered exoskeleton is to reduce the impedance of the human-exoskeleton system through an appropriate control strategy (more benificial)
- 2. Compensate or amplify the wearer's motor ability to achieve a desired movement


**Experiment**

- RMS of the `EMG` signals of the muscels involved in the flexion/extension was used as a metric
- `EMG` signal levels can be efficiently reduced by inertia, damping, and gravity compensation compared with those obtained without wearing the
exoskeleton

### **2. Methodology:**  
---
#### **Human-Exoskeleton Model**: (Model Based)

Impedance control overall equation:

The dynamic model of an $n$-DOF robotic manipulator interacting with the environment can be expressed as

$$ M(q)\ddot{q} + C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q} + G(q) + \tau_f = \tau + J^T F $$

where:
- $q$: represents the state vector of joint angles. 
- $M(q)$: inertia matrix of the robot, dependent on the joint configuration $q$.
- $\ddot{q}$: joint acceleration vector.
- $C(q,\dot{q})$: Coriolis and centrifugal matrix.
- $C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q}$: Coriolis and centrifugal torque vector.
- $G(q)$: gravity torque vector.
- $\tau_f$: joint friction torque vector.
- $\tau$: control input vector , this include:  
    - $\tau_h$: the human joint torque
    - $\tau_e$: the torque generated by the exoskeleton
    > $\tau$ = $\tau_h$ + $\tau_e$
- $J$: Jacobian matrix of the manipulator.
- $J^T$: transpose of the Jacobian matrix.
- $F$: external force/torque vector acting on the end-effector.
- $J^T F$: generalized joint torque induced by the external Cartesian force.

**Proof - or can be seen as explanation:**:  

The dynamic model of an $n$-DOF robotic manipulator interacting with the environment is given by

$$M(q)\ddot{q} + C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q} + G(q) + \tau_f = \tau + J^T F$$

Although this equation may appear complicated, each term has a clear physical interpretation and can be related to familiar concepts from classical mechanics.

**Correspondence with Classical Physics**

| Robot Dynamics | Classical Physics Equivalent | Physical Meaning |
|----------------|------------------------------|------------------|
| $M(q)\ddot{q}$ | $ma$ | Inertia |
| $C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q}$ | $B\dot{x}$ | Damping / Viscous resistance |
| $G(q)$ | $mg$ | Gravity |
| $\tau_f$ | $F_f$ | Friction |
| $\tau$ | $F_{control}$ | Control input |
| $J^T F$ | $F_{external}$ | External force from the environment |

---

**Inertia Term**

According to Newton's Second Law,

$$
F = ma
$$

The robot counterpart is

$$M(q)\ddot{q}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;ma$$

where:

- $M(q)$ is the inertia matrix of the robot.
- $\ddot{q}$ is the joint acceleration.

This term represents the resistance of the robot against acceleration. A larger inertia means that more torque is required to produce the same acceleration.

---

**Damping Term**

In classical mechanics, damping is often modeled as

$$F_d = B\dot{x}$$

where:

- $B$ is the damping coefficient.
- $\dot{x}$ is the velocity.

The corresponding robot term is

$$C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;B\dot{x}$$

Physically, this term opposes motion and dissipates energy, similar to viscous friction in a mechanical system.

---

**Gravity Term**

The gravity contribution can be related to

$$F_g = mg$$

which corresponds to

$$G(q)$$

in robot dynamics.

This term compensates for the weight of the robot links and payload.

---

**Stiffness Term**

The concept of stiffness comes from Hooke's Law:

$$F_s = -K(x-x_0)$$

where:

- $K$ is the stiffness coefficient.
- $x_0$ is the equilibrium position.

The larger the value of $K$, the stronger the restoring force that pulls the system back toward its equilibrium position.

---

**Intuitive Interpretation**

Instead of thinking about matrices and robot dynamics, impedance control can be viewed as assigning a desired mechanical behavior to the robot.

For example, choosing

- a large $M_d$ makes the robot feel "heavy",
- a large $B_d$ makes the robot move more smoothly with less oscillation,
- a large $K_d$ makes the robot behave more rigidly.

In other words, impedance control allows the designer to specify:

> "I want the robot end-effector to behave like a virtual object with a certain mass, damping, and stiffness."

This physical intuition is the foundation of impedance control and explains why the controller is often described as creating a virtual mass-spring-damper system between the robot and its environment.
